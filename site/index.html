Methodology & verification

What moves, mechanically. For an enterprise DCF the scenario rate enters through CAPM: cost of equity = Rf + beta × 4.5%, blended at the model's own capital-structure weights with its after-tax cost of debt held fixed, giving WACC(Rf). The model's projected free cash flows (2026–2030) are discounted at that WACC, a Gordon terminal value is struck at the selected terminal growth, net debt is bridged out, and the result is divided by shares outstanding. For the four financials modelled on distributable cash flow (JPM, GS, MS, BLK), the same cash flows are discounted at the cost of equity directly and there is no debt bridge — exactly as in the published workbooks.

How the inputs were extracted. Each teardown workbook was built by a Python script whose mirror function reproduces the workbook's arithmetic. I executed a copy of each company's own build script, recovered its base-case cash-flow stream, beta, share count, net debt and discount-rate build, and stored them in this site's data file — the source models were never modified.

The verification gate. Before a company was allowed to use the exact repricing above, my recomputation at the published settings (Rf 5.1%, terminal growth 3.0%) had to reproduce its published base-case value within 1%. All 20 companies passed; the worst deviation is 0.015% (PLTR), pure rounding from the published two-decimal values. A second, independent check recalculated all 20 workbooks in LibreOffice and tied every DCF_Base per-share cell back to the published figure — again 20 of 20. The full per-company table is in VERIFICATION.md in the project repository. Had a company failed the gate, it would have been repriced by interpolation across its published bear/base/bull values and flagged here; none needed it.

What this shows. At the published 5.1% the lab reproduces the teardown's calls exactly — 1 BUY, 2 HOLD, 17 PASS, median implied upside −46.5%. Cut the 10Y to 4.0% and the median improves only to −40.1% (1 BUY, 3 HOLD, 16 PASS): even a full point of rate relief does not rescue a universe priced for flawless growth, because most of the gap sits in the cash-flow expectations, not the discount rate. The most rate-sensitive names are the long-duration, low-current-yield ones — SCHW and NOW each gain ~23% of fair value on that one-point move; the least sensitive are the high-beta names whose cost of equity is dominated by the beta term (COIN, ALAB, ~7%).

Limitations

· Cash flows are frozen. Only the discount rate and terminal growth move. In reality a 200 bp rate shift would change growth, margins, multiples and the forecasts themselves — this isolates the pure discounting channel, nothing more.

· Cost of debt is held fixed at each model's after-tax figure. A fuller treatment would reprice debt with the risk-free rate too; holding it fixed slightly understates sensitivity for levered names.

· Betas, weights and share counts are as of the model date (October 19, 2026) and do not update.

· Prices refresh weekly from the Sector Teardown feed, so implied upsides drift with the market between visits while fair values stay anchored to the published teardown models.

· DCF values are scenario arithmetic on my own published assumptions, not predictions. Reasonable analysts will disagree with the inputs; the workbooks behind every number are public in the Sector Teardown repository.
